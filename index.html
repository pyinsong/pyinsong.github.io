  • Time-window decomposition — Raw bureau variables often report fixed 24-month aggregates. Decomposing into sub-windows (3, 6, 12, 18, 24 months) generates temporal granularity: a customer with all delinquencies in months 19–24 ago appears identical to one with recent DPD events on a raw 24-month count, but their risk profiles are fundamentally different. From a modeling theory standpoint, narrower windows proxy for hazard rate intensity — the instantaneous rate of a credit event per unit exposure time — making the features more predictive for shorter-horizon outcomes. Applied systematically, one raw variable (e.g., num_DPD_trades) yields 4–5 engineered features across windows, each carrying non-redundant temporal signal. · the primary lever for extracting value from standard bureau trade-line data
  • Rolling statistics (mean, max, std, trend) — For sequential or monthly bureau snapshots, compute rolling aggregations over multiple lookback windows: rolling mean balance (stability of spending), rolling max balance (peak exposure), rolling std of payment amounts (volatility). The ratio rolling_mean_3m / rolling_mean_12m captures trend direction without requiring explicit trend modeling; values > 1 indicate increasing balance trajectory. Standard deviation captures behavioral volatility — high variance in payment history often signals financial stress independent of the mean payment level. · most powerful when month-level bureau trade-line history is available
  • Ratio and utilization features — Absolute values are frequently dominated by credit limit size, income level, or portfolio tenure, making them poor cross-customer comparators. Ratio features normalize for these confounders: credit utilization = revolving_balance / revolving_limit (captures constraint independently of balance magnitude); payment ratio = last_payment / min_payment_due (captures payment behavior independently of balance size); delinquency rate = DPD_count_6m / total_open_trades (captures propensity independently of portfolio size). Ratios are typically among the top-ranked features by gain/SHAP in both LR and XGBoost because they encode relative behavior, which generalizes better across customer segments. · always engineer utilization-style ratios before fitting; they compress two raw features into one more predictive signal
  • Recency features — Months since last delinquent event (MSLDE), months since last inquiry, months since last new account opened. Raw count features cannot distinguish whether events occurred yesterday or two years ago. Recency features expose the temporal decay of risk signal — a delinquency 30 months ago is categorically different from one 3 months ago, but raw 24-month counts treat them equivalently. Directly analogous to the Recency component in RFM (Recency-Frequency-Magnitude) from direct marketing analytics, where recency of purchase is often the single strongest predictor of future response. · cross-domain technique: same logic applies to customer engagement models (days since last email open, last purchase)
  • Velocity and delta features — Month-over-month or quarter-over-quarter change in balance, utilization, number of open accounts. Velocity features capture trajectory, which static snapshot features miss: a customer whose utilization increased from 20% to 80% over 6 months has a fundamentally different risk profile than one who has held 80% utilization steadily, despite identical current values. In tree models, delta features frequently rank in the top decile by gain because they expose non-linear inflection patterns that require multi-node splits on raw features to approximate. · effective for detecting recent behavioral shifts before they manifest in delinquency
  • Interaction and cross features — Manually constructed products or combinations of two variables: (DPD_count_6m × avg_balance_6m) encodes severity-weighted delinquency; (utilization_ratio × months_since_account_open) captures how quickly a new account was maxed out. Cross features encode domain hypotheses about which variable combinations are jointly meaningful. This is especially important for Logistic Regression, which is a strictly additive model and cannot learn feature interactions on its own — any non-additive signal must be explicitly engineered. For XGBoost, cross features can still accelerate learning by giving the model a compressed shortcut to a joint relationship that would otherwise require deep trees to discover. · mandatory for LR; a modeling efficiency gain for tree models
  • Binary derogatory flags — Convert continuous event counts into binary indicators: has_any_60dpd_12m, has_collection_24m, has_public_record, has_chargeoff_ever. In many credit and marketing propensity models, the presence or absence of a derogatory event is more predictive than its count — the distinction between zero and one derogatory is far larger than the distinction between three and four. Binary flags often achieve higher IV than the underlying count variable and are more stable across portfolio vintages. In LR scorecards, WoE-encoded binary flags frequently rank as top-3 predictors. · always test binary version alongside count version; let IV and AUC on OOT data decide
  • WoE transformation (LR-specific) — Replace each feature's raw values with its Weight of Evidence: WoE_bin = ln(P(Y=0 | bin) / P(Y=1 | bin)). This transformation maps features onto the log-odds scale, which is exactly the scale of LR's linear predictor (logit link). The practical effect: WoE-transformed features satisfy LR's linearity-in-log-odds assumption by construction, eliminating the need to manually specify a functional form. WoE also compresses high-cardinality or non-monotonic raw features into smooth monotonic bins, making the model more interpretable and adversarial to outliers. Applied after FE and before model fitting for LR-based scorecards. · not applicable to tree models, which handle non-linearity natively
  • IV / WoE filter (pre-modeling) — First pass on 1,000–2,000+ raw bureau features. IV = Σ(%Good − %Bad) × WoE, where WoE = ln(%Good / %Bad) per bin. IV > 0.3 is strong in credit applications; WoE transformation simultaneously linearizes the feature-to-log-odds relationship, directly satisfying LR's linearity assumption. Cuts the candidate set by 60–80% before any regularization. · de facto first step in credit scorecard development
  • L1 regularization (Lasso) — Appends λΣ|βⱼ| to the log-loss objective. The absolute-value penalty creates a non-differentiable "corner" at zero in the constraint geometry, forcing sparse solutions where many βⱼ are driven exactly to zero. λ controls sparsity and is tuned via k-fold CV on AUC/KS. Coordinate descent solves the resulting non-smooth optimization efficiently at high dimensionality. Typical result: 1,000+ raw features → <50 non-zero coefficients. · primary automated selector for high-dimensional LR
  • L2 regularization (Ridge) — Appends λΣβⱼ² to the objective. Quadratic penalty shrinks all coefficients toward zero but never to exactly zero — it is a stabilizer, not a selector. Key benefit: analytically reduces the impact of multicollinearity by adding λI to the (XᵀX) matrix before inversion, preventing inflated and sign-flipped coefficients from correlated bureau features. Used as a baseline comparison or combined with L1 as Elastic Net. · especially useful when most features carry genuine signal
  • Elastic Net (L1 + L2) — Objective penalty: αλΣ|βⱼ| + (1−α)λΣβⱼ². Pure Lasso arbitrarily picks one feature from a correlated group and discards the rest; Elastic Net tends to include or exclude correlated features together (grouping property), which is preferable when multiple trade-line variables from the same bureau account carry redundant but complementary signal. α is tuned alongside λ via grid search CV. · preferred for datasets with many correlated feature clusters
  • Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) — Pre-regularization multicollinearity screen. VIF_j = 1 / (1 − R²_j), where R²_j is the coefficient of determination from regressing feature j on all other features. VIF > 10 indicates near-perfect collinearity; the feature's coefficient estimate is numerically unstable. Applied iteratively: remove the highest-VIF feature, recompute, repeat until all VIF < 10. Critical before LR because OLS/MLE estimation of β directly inverts (XᵀX); near-singular matrices produce large, unreliable standard errors. · must precede regularized LR to prevent inflated penalty on correlated features
  • Stability Selection (bootstrap Lasso) — Run Lasso across B bootstrap sub-samples of the training data. A feature's selection probability = fraction of runs where its coefficient is non-zero. Retain only features with selection probability > 0.5. Controls the expected number of false discoveries (E[V] ≤ q²/((2·π_thr − 1)·p) per Meinshausen & Bühlmann, 2010). More robust than single-run Lasso, which can vary significantly with sampling noise in a 1,000+ feature space. · trending in high-dimensional credit feature spaces
  • Gain-based importance (built-in default) — For each candidate split on feature j, XGBoost computes: Gain = ½ · [G_L²/(H_L+λ) + G_R²/(H_R+λ) − (G_L+G_R)²/(H_L+H_R+λ)] − γ, where G, H are first- and second-order gradient statistics (∂L/∂ŷ and ∂²L/∂ŷ²) summed over observations in each node, λ is the L2 leaf-weight regularization term, and γ is the minimum gain threshold for a split to occur. A feature's importance = average gain across all splits where it is used. Directly measures reduction in the differentiable objective function — the most theoretically grounded of XGBoost's native metrics. · primary ranking metric; however, can overweight features used in few high-gain splits
  • Cover & Frequency as complementary views — Cover = average number of training samples (weighted by second-order gradient H) routed through a feature's splits; Frequency = raw count of split appearances across all trees. Gain can be dominated by a feature that appears in only a handful of splits with outsized impact; comparing all three surfaces features that are influential but data-sparse (potential overfit signal). A high-gain, very-low-cover feature warrants scrutiny. · use all three together; divergence between metrics is informative
  • SHAP values (SHapley Additive exPlanations) — Grounded in cooperative game theory: SHAP assigns each feature a contribution φⱼ satisfying efficiency (Σφⱼ = ŷ − E[ŷ]), symmetry, linearity, and the dummy axiom. Critically, SHAP is consistent: if a model changes so that a feature contributes less in every context, its SHAP importance cannot increase — a property that gain-based metrics violate. TreeSHAP (Lundberg et al., 2018) computes exact Shapley values for tree ensembles in polynomial time O(TLD²). In practice: rank features by mean |SHAP| for global importance; use SHAP dependence plots to detect interaction effects and non-monotonic relationships; use beeswarm plots to identify features that may need binning or capping before adding to a scorecard. · the current industry standard for explainable feature selection in regulated models
  • Permutation Importance — Shuffle a single feature's values across the held-out set (breaking its association with the target) and measure the resulting AUC/KS drop. Captures marginal contribution inclusive of feature interactions, which gain-based metrics miss (gain attributes interaction credit only to the split that makes the final partition). Model-agnostic — applied post-training without retraining. Computationally O(features × scoring passes). Useful for validating that top gain features hold up under a stricter test. · good sanity check for features suspected of being correlated proxies
  • Boruta algorithm — Statistical wrapper around XGBoost: create a "shadow" version of each feature by randomly shuffling its values, append shadows to the feature matrix, train XGBoost on the combined set. A real feature must achieve consistently higher importance (gain or SHAP) than the maximum shadow feature importance to be confirmed relevant. Repeat over many iterations; apply a two-sided binomial test to classify features as Confirmed, Tentative, or Rejected. More principled than a fixed top-N cutoff because it explicitly controls the false-discovery rate. · trending; preferred when a principled reject/accept decision is required
  • Correlation-based pruning after initial ranking — After selecting top-N by SHAP/gain, compute pairwise Spearman rank correlations. For pairs above 0.85–0.90, drop the feature with lower mean |SHAP|. In boosted trees, correlated features split the importance credit across both; removing the weaker one rarely hurts AUC but reduces model complexity and PSI risk. · keeps the final feature set parsimonious without the cost of RFE
  • Recursive Feature Elimination (RFE) with XGBoost — Train on all features, remove the lowest-importance feature (or bottom k%), retrain, repeat. Track the AUC/KS vs. feature count curve and identify the elbow — the point where further reduction begins to cost measurable performance. Expensive (O(features) training runs) but identifies the minimum-size feature set at near-optimal performance. · use when scorecard parsimony is a hard requirement
  • PSI-gated selection — Before finalizing any feature, compute its PSI between the training window and a held-out out-of-time validation window: PSI = Σ(%Actual − %Expected) × ln(%Actual / %Expected). PSI > 0.25 is a red flag; the feature has shifted distribution and will degrade out-of-time performance regardless of training-set importance. Unstable features are excluded even if they show top-decile gain/SHAP. · prevents selecting a feature that fits history but fails in production
  • KS Statistic — primary separation metric in credit card modeling; measures max distance between cumulative good/bad distributions across score bins
  • AUC-ROC & Gini coefficient — Gini = 2×AUC − 1; threshold-independent rank-ordering performance
  • Lift curves & decile / gains analysis — "top 2 deciles capture X% of responders" — the language of marketing campaign targeting decisions
  • Calibration & reliability diagrams — ensures predicted probabilities are well-calibrated when used as continuous scores for cutoff optimization
  • PSI for model stability monitoring — tracks score distribution shift between development and production populations over time
  • Precision / Recall / F1 & log loss — used for threshold selection and probabilistic model comparison
  • Hypothesis framing — null / alternative hypothesis, one-tailed vs. two-tailed tests; Type I (false positive, α) and Type II (false negative, β) error trade-offs
  • Sample size & power calculation — pre-experiment sizing using effect size, α, and target power (1−β); prevents underpowered tests that produce inconclusive results
  • p-value vs. confidence interval interpretation — CI-first reporting avoids binary pass/fail thinking; quantifies magnitude and uncertainty of lift
  • Multiple testing correction — Bonferroni for conservative control; Benjamini-Hochberg (FDR) when testing many segments simultaneously
  • Stratified & matched randomization — pre-stratify on key covariates (risk tier, channel, vintage) to reduce variance and prevent imbalanced test/control splits
  • Holdout group design — permanent holdouts for long-running champion-challenger model evaluation; distinguishes model lift from campaign lift
  • Incremental lift measurement — isolates treatment effect from organic behavior; bridges A/B testing into uplift modeling for targeting persuadables only
  • Sequential testing & early stopping rules — alpha-spending functions (O'Brien-Fleming) to allow interim looks without inflating Type I error
